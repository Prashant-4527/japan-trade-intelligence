## Key Finding 1 - The 2022 Energy-Currency Crisis

Japan recorded its largest trade deficit of the decade in 2022, 
reaching -$152bn. This crisis resulted from two simultaneous shocks:
the yen falling to 32-year low, making all imports more expensive,
and Russia's invasion of Ukraine driving gloabl energy price sharply upward. Because Japan imports approximately 90% of its energy need, both shock compunded - Japan paid more yen per barrel AND more dollars per barrel for the same energy at the same time. By 2023 the deficit narrowed to -$34bn as energy prices stabilized, but Japan's structural exposure to this exact scenario remains unchanged.



## Key Finding 2 — China Dependency and the Disruption Paradox

China supplies 22.4% of Japan's total imports ($168bn) — more than double the 10% threshold economists flag as high-risk, and Japan's 
only partner above 20%. Unlike Australia or the USA, China is a geopolitical rival rather than an ally, making this concentration strategically significant. Japan's HHI concentration score of 827 technically falls in the "unconcentrated" category, but this is misleading: Japan trades with 220 countries, 200 of which contribute less than 1% each and cannot realistically replace China's supply if disrupted. China's import share has remained stuck between 21-26% for the full decade, suggesting Japan's 2021 Economic Security Promotion Act has not yet reduced this dependency in practice. A 
supply disruption simulation reveals a paradox: if China reduced exports by 30%, Japan's trade balance would actually IMPROVE to a 
+$16.6bn surplus — because the import bill shrinks faster than export revenue changes — while the economy absorbs a 6% GDP supply 
shock as factories lose access to critical components. A healthier trade balance during a supply crisis is not good news; it reflects 
Japan's inability to purchase what it needs.



## Key Finding 3 — Export Stagnation and Sector Concentration

Using 2014 as a baseline (=100), Japan's export growth index reached only 104 by 2023 — the second-slowest among the six economies 
analyzed, compared to China's 144. In dollar terms, Japan added just $28bn in export value over the decade while China added $1,037bn. 
This stagnation is partly a currency illusion: the yen fell from ¥105/dollar in 2014 to ¥140/dollar in 2023, so even if export volume 
grew, the same goods convert to fewer dollars — masking real growth in USD-denominated comparisons. More concerning is sector 
concentration: of Japan's six major export sectors, only Automotive (+10.4%) grew above its 2014 level, while Electronics, Machinery, 
Steel, and Chemicals all declined — Chemicals by 37%. Japan's entire export growth currently depends on one sector, and that sector faces 
a direct existential threat: as Tesla and BYD accelerate the global shift to EVs, Japan's hybrid/ICE-based automotive strength could 
become its next point of decline rather than its only source of growth.



## Key Finding 4 - The Widening Future (forecast)

Export growth trend:  +$8.85bn per year
Import growth trend: +$12.47bn per year
Gap: imports growing $3.62bn FASTER than exports every year

Projected:
2024: -$47.8bn deficit
2025: -$51.5bn deficit
2026: -$55.1bn deficit

Japan's deficit is structural and widening. Under the current trajectory, Japan will be running a -$55bn deficit by 2026 with no reversal in sight.
The honest limitation: This is a linear projection. It assumes tomorrow looks like yesterday. It does not account for: yen recovery, BOJ interest rate changes, US tariffs on Japanese goods, EV disruption of Automotive, nuclear restarts reducing energy imports, or any geopolitical shock. These could all bend the line significantly in either direction.
